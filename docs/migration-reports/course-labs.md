## Corrections supported by the data and current APIs

1. **The sample times are not uniform as stored.** The original `dt=t[2]-t[1]` is 0.009 s, but the file starts with a 0.046 s interval and then alternates approximately 0.008/0.009 s because of timestamp rounding. Both new notebooks show all interval diagnostics and use the measured times for numerical differentiation. The filters operate on an explicitly interpolated uniform grid over the continuous post-gap interval. It has 125 points from 0.046 to 1.080 s, yielding `fs=119.92263056092841 Hz`, rather than the original implied 111.111 Hz. The initial excluded point remains present in the raw analysis. Large internal gaps are rejected with an explanatory error instead of silently being filtered as uniform data.
2. **No artificial final zero velocity.** The original loop allocated N zeros but filled only N−1. It now returns N−1 interval velocities, plotted at interval midpoints. The loop and `np.diff(x)/np.diff(t)` are asserted equal. `np.gradient(..., t, edge_order=2)` is a separate N-point derivative estimate; its edge behavior is explained. Context7 `/websites/numpy_doc_2_4` verified [gradient](https://numpy.org/doc/stable/reference/generated/numpy.gradient.html) and [diff](https://numpy.org/doc/stable/reference/generated/numpy.diff.html).
3. **Gravity and boundary interpretation.** With y positive upward, `ay=-g` in ideal free flight, including at the apex. The original first-ten-sample exclusion is preserved and compared with exclusion at both ends. Neither is described as a universal valid boundary rule. The new quadratic-model comparison makes its constant-acceleration/free-flight assumption explicit and shows residuals. For the original file, the interior mean of the raw timestamp-based second derivative gives `g=9.839142255473226 m/s²`; a quadratic fit to all positions gives `9.898625934888397 m/s²`. The difference is `0.059483679415171 m/s²`.
4. **Moving-average startup is not an algorithm disagreement.** The loop preserves the initial three values, as before, whereas `lfilter` with no initial state assumes zero prior samples. They agree from index 3 onward, checked in the notebook. A constant input of 8 gives initial causal outputs 2,4,6,8. The redundant denominator `[1,0,0,0]` is simplified to `[1]` without changing the FIR.
5. **Bidirectional filtering changes magnitude too.** The prose now explains noncausality, boundary effects and the squared magnitude response. A four-point causal moving average has a low-frequency group delay of 1.5 samples. Third-order low-pass Butterworth applied forward and backward has effective order 6 and about −6 dB gain at its design cutoff. The Butterworth examples use `butter(..., fs=fs, output='sos')` with `sosfiltfilt`; the educational FIR comparison retains `lfilter` and `filtfilt`. Context7 `/scipy/scipy` supplied the current [butter](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.signal.butter.html), [filtfilt](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.signal.filtfilt.html) and [sosfiltfilt](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.signal.sosfiltfilt.html) documentation.
6. **Residual analysis is a model-based suggestion.** The old `!pip install` is replaced with `optcutfreq>=0.0.10` metadata. The notebooks plot the residual analysis and handle a `None` result explicitly. Context7 resolved `/demotu/optcutfreq` and verified the [maintainer examples and limitations](https://github.com/demotu/optcutfreq). The local installed signature was inspected as well: `(y, freq=1, fclim=None, show=False, ax=None)`. The original course data on the declared uniform grid produces a suggested cutoff of `6.267507748757586 Hz` in the checked environment; it is not claimed to be a universally correct cutoff or equivalent to the N=1 teaching example.
